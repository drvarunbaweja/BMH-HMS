👁 What is a Cataract?

A cataract (Motiaabind / Chitta Motia) is a clouding of the eye's natural crystalline lens. Light cannot pass through clearly, causing progressively blurred, faded, or glare-affected vision.

The lens sits behind the iris (coloured part). When it clouds, the only cure is surgical removal and replacement with an artificial lens (IOL).

  • Symptoms: blurry vision, halos around lights, faded colours, difficulty reading or driving at night
  • Cause: ageing (most common), diabetes, steroids, UV exposure, trauma
  • Eye drops cannot dissolve a cataract — surgery is the definitive treatment

🔭 Intraocular Lens (IOL) Choices — Which is Right for You?
IOL Type What it Corrects Glasses Needed? Ideal For Grade
Monofocal Standard Distance or near (one focal point) Yes — for the uncorrected distance (reading or driving) Budget-conscious; no astigmatism; accepts glasses Standard
Toric IOL Distance + astigmatism correction Glasses-free for distance; reading glasses needed Astigmatism ≥0.75D; wants good distance without glasses Enhanced
Multifocal IOL Distance + near (two focal points) Minimal — most daily tasks glasses-free Active lifestyle; willing to adapt to some halos/glare initially Premium
EDOF (Extended Depth) Excellent distance + intermediate; near with glasses Reading glasses for fine print only Computer users; less glare than multifocal Premium
Trifocal Premium
(e.g. Alcon PanOptix, Zeiss AT Triton)		 Distance + intermediate + near (three focal points) Spectacle independence in 95% of patients Best possible freedom from glasses; premium budget Best Choice
Toric Trifocal Distance + intermediate + near + astigmatism Near-complete independence; fine print only Astigmatism + desire for full spectacle freedom Best Choice
* Final IOL choice is made after biometry, topography, and pupil dilation assessment. Not all IOLs are suitable for every eye.
🏥 The PMICS Procedure — Step by Step
  • 1 Dilating drops instilled — pupil widens to expose lens
  • 2 Topical anaesthesia — eye-drop numbing, no injection needed
  • 3 2 mm self-sealing incision at corneal margin — no stitch
  • 4 Phacoemulsification — ultrasound probe breaks up cloudy lens
  • 5 Foldable IOL injected through the tiny incision; unfolds in eye
  • 6 Wound self-seals; antibiotic drops given; eye shield fitted
  • 7 Total time: 15–20 minutes · Same-day discharge
⚠️ Rare Risks (Overall <1%)
  • Posterior capsule rupture — vitreous loss; managed intraoperatively; vision usually preserved
  • Infection (endophthalmitis) — very rare (<0.05%); treated with antibiotic injections
  • Raised IOP / corneal swelling — usually transient; managed with drops
  • Posterior capsule opacification (PCO) — cloudy membrane develops months later; treated with 5-minute YAG laser
✅ Post-op Instructions
  • Antibiotic drops: 4×/day for 4 weeks
  • Steroid drops: 4× tapering to 1× over 6 weeks
  • NSAID drops: 3× for 4 weeks (prevents macular oedema)
  • Do not rub the eye; use shield at night for 1 week
  • No swimming / heavy lifting for 4 weeks
  • Dark glasses outdoors for 2 weeks
  • Vision may be hazy day 1 — clears by day 2–3
  • Final glasses prescription after 4–6 weeks
📅 Follow-up Schedule
  • 📍 Day 1 — IOP, corneal clarity, vision check
  • 📍 Week 1 — wound integrity, drops review
  • 📍 Month 1 — refraction, IOP, taper drops
  • 📍 Month 3 & 1 year — final glasses, YAG if needed
Seek urgent review if: sudden pain, vision loss, severe redness, or seeing flashes/floaters in the operated eye
